Nurses set up a folding table in a Middletown elementary school gymnasium on a recent weekday morning, a small station stocked with vaccine coolers and consent forms that has become a fixture of a new outreach campaign aimed at closing gaps in childhood immunization rates. The effort brings routine vaccinations directly into school buildings, an approach organizers say removes some of the most common barriers that keep families from staying current on recommended shots.

The push comes amid broader concern among Connecticut pediatricians about uneven vaccination coverage, particularly among families who face transportation challenges, inconsistent work schedules, or difficulty securing timely appointments at crowded pediatric practices. School-based clinics allow children to receive routine immunizations during the school day, with parental consent secured in advance through paperwork sent home ahead of each visit.

## Meeting Families Where They Already Are

Public health nurses coordinating the program say the logic behind bringing vaccines into schools is straightforward: attendance is already mandatory, so a clinic set up during the school day reaches nearly every enrolled student without requiring an additional trip or missed workday for parents.

"A lot of families want their kids vaccinated on schedule, but life gets in the way," said a school nurse involved in coordinating the clinics. "If we can get consent forms signed and administer the vaccine right here during the school day, we remove a lot of the logistical hurdles that cause delays."

The program has focused initial outreach on vaccines required for school enrollment as well as recommended seasonal immunizations, coordinating closely with families' existing pediatricians to ensure records stay current and consistent across providers. Nurses working the clinics upload documentation directly into the state's immunization registry, reducing the paperwork burden on both families and school administrators.

## Addressing Access Gaps Beyond the Classroom

Outreach organizers say the school-based model has proven particularly valuable for families without a consistent primary care provider, a gap that has grown in parts of the state as some pediatric practices have closed or stopped accepting new patients amid provider shortages. For these families, the school clinic sometimes represents the most reliable point of contact with the healthcare system a child has all year.

The initiative has also included a parent education component, with informational materials distributed in multiple languages addressing common questions and concerns about vaccine safety and scheduling. Organizers say building trust with families who have questions is treated as seriously as the logistics of running the clinic itself, with nurses trained to answer questions patiently rather than simply administering shots on a tight schedule.

## Early Results and Next Steps

Early participation numbers from the first round of school clinics have been encouraging, according to program staff, though officials caution that measuring the program's full effect on community-wide vaccination rates will take more time and data. Plans are already underway to expand the model to additional schools across Middlesex County in the coming school year, with organizers hoping to eventually make the outreach a recurring annual fixture rather than a pilot program.

For parents like one Middletown mother of two, who signed consent forms for her children to receive updated immunizations during the first round of clinics, the convenience made a meaningful difference. "I've been meaning to get them in for months," she said. "Having it happen right at school meant it actually got done."
