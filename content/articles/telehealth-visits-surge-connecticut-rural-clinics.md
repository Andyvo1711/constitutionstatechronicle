What began as a stopgap measure has settled into a routine part of everyday medicine across Connecticut. New scheduling data reviewed by several community health centers shows that virtual visits now account for a meaningful share of primary care appointments statewide, with the sharpest growth occurring not in the state's cities but in its smaller towns.

Clinics in eastern Connecticut, where specialists can be scarce and driving distances long, report that video and phone consultations have become especially popular for medication check-ins, mental health follow-ups, and chronic disease management. Patients who once had to take a half-day off work for a routine appointment can now log in from a break room or a kitchen table.

## A Shift From Novelty to Necessity

For much of the past decade, telehealth was treated as an add-on service, offered mainly for convenience. That has changed. A physician assistant at a community health center in Windham County said virtual visits now make up a substantial share of her daily schedule, particularly among patients managing hypertension or diabetes who need frequent but brief check-ins.

"For a lot of our patients, especially the ones juggling two jobs, the alternative to a telehealth visit isn't an in-person visit," she said. "It's no visit at all. That's the real comparison we should be making."

Clinic administrators describe a similar pattern among older patients, many of whom were initially hesitant about video calls but have since grown comfortable with tablets provided through state and grant-funded digital literacy programs. Several clinics have hired part-time staff specifically to help elderly patients get set up for their first appointment.

## Reimbursement and Infrastructure Questions Remain

The growth of telehealth in Connecticut has not been without friction. Broadband access remains inconsistent in parts of the state's rural northeast and northwest corners, forcing some clinics to fall back on phone-only consultations that limit what a provider can assess. Reimbursement policies for virtual visits, set through a mix of state Medicaid rules and private insurer contracts, have also shifted multiple times in recent years, creating administrative headaches for smaller practices trying to plan staffing around them.

Behavioral health providers say telehealth has been particularly valuable for expanding access to therapy and psychiatric care, fields where Connecticut has faced provider shortages for years. A licensed clinical social worker who splits her caseload between in-person and virtual sessions said remote appointments have allowed her to reach patients in towns where no therapist previously accepted new clients.

## Looking Ahead to Hybrid Care Models

Health system planners across the state say they expect telehealth to remain a permanent option rather than a pandemic-era exception, with most providers now designing hybrid models that mix video visits with periodic in-person exams. Community health centers in particular are investing in remote monitoring tools, such as connected blood pressure cuffs and glucose meters, that let providers track patients between virtual appointments.

Not every type of care translates well to a screen, and providers are careful to note that certain diagnoses, physical exams, and procedures still require an office visit. But for routine follow-ups and medication management, many Connecticut clinicians now describe telehealth as simply another tool in the exam room, one that has proven especially valuable for patients in the state's more rural and underserved communities. As broadband expansion projects slowly reach more of the state's rural stretches, clinic administrators expect the technology's reach, and its role in everyday care, to keep growing.
