Inside a converted farmhouse on the edge of Glastonbury's town center, Stillwater Holistic Center houses an unusual combination of practitioners under one roof: a licensed acupuncturist, a meditation instructor, a nutrition coach, and a massage therapist, all working from a shared intake process designed to treat each client as a single case rather than a set of separate appointments.

"So often, holistic wellness gets treated as a buffet where you pick one thing and stick with it," said founder Renata Wu, who trained in traditional Chinese medicine before opening the center five years ago. "We wanted to build something where the different disciplines actually talk to each other."

## An Integrated Intake Process

Every new client at Stillwater begins with a ninety-minute consultation involving at least two practitioners, who together develop a plan that might combine acupuncture for a physical complaint with a meditation practice aimed at an underlying stress pattern the client identifies during intake. Wu said the model requires far more coordination than a typical wellness practice, including weekly staff meetings where practitioners discuss shared clients.

"It would be much simpler to just run four separate businesses under one roof," Wu said. "But the value we offer comes specifically from the practitioners communicating with each other about a client's progress."

The center's meditation instructor, Callum Ferris, said the collaborative model has changed how he approaches his own sessions. "I used to just teach breathing techniques in isolation," he said. "Now I know if a client is also dealing with chronic pain that the acupuncturist is treating, and I can tailor a practice that actually supports that work instead of existing separately from it."

## Navigating Skepticism and Building Trust

Wu acknowledged that the center's integrative approach can be a difficult sell to clients accustomed to more conventional, single-discipline wellness providers, and that some prospective clients remain skeptical of acupuncture or meditation as anything beyond a relaxation exercise. She said the center addresses this by leaning heavily on client education, including printed materials explaining the research behind each practice offered.

Client Isabelle Torrance, a Glastonbury resident who came to Stillwater seeking help with chronic migraines after conventional treatments provided only partial relief, said the combination of approaches made a difference she had not expected.

"I went in expecting the acupuncture to be the main event," Torrance said. "But it was actually the nutrition coaching, paired with the acupuncture, that helped me figure out a trigger I'd never identified before."

Stillwater has grown gradually since opening, adding its nutrition coaching program two years ago after client demand made clear that dietary factors were coming up repeatedly during intake sessions. Wu said she is now considering adding a yoga therapy component, though she is deliberately taking her time with the decision.

"I would rather grow slowly and keep the coordination between practitioners intact than expand quickly and lose what makes this place work," she said. Stillwater Holistic Center currently accepts new clients by consultation only, with a waitlist that Wu said typically runs several weeks during the fall and winter months.
