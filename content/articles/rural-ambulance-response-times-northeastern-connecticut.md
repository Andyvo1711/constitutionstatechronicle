For decades, emergency medical coverage in much of northeastern Connecticut has depended on a shrinking pool of volunteers willing to carry a pager and drop what they're doing when it goes off. In towns across the Quiet Corner, that model is showing serious strain, and several municipalities have begun weighing whether to shift toward paid ambulance services, a change that would mark a significant departure from the region's longstanding approach to emergency care.

Volunteer EMS squads have covered much of rural Windham County for generations, staffed largely by residents who complete emergency medical technician training on their own time and respond to calls between work and family obligations. But volunteer rosters have thinned considerably in recent years, driven by an aging population of longtime volunteers, demanding certification renewal requirements, and a shrinking pool of younger residents willing or able to commit the necessary hours.

## When Minutes Matter

Response time is not an abstract statistic in emergency medicine; it can be the difference between a full recovery and a permanent injury in situations like cardiac arrest or severe trauma. Several towns in the region have seen average response times creep upward as squads struggle to maintain full daytime coverage, a period when many volunteers are at their regular jobs, often outside the town they serve.

A longtime volunteer EMT in one Windham County town described the strain plainly. "We used to have enough people that someone was almost always available during the day," he said. "Now there are stretches where we're waiting to see if anyone can even respond, and if not, we're calling for mutual aid from the next town over, which adds precious minutes."

Mutual aid agreements, in which neighboring towns' squads respond when a local unit is unavailable, have become an increasingly routine part of covering gaps, but officials note that relying on mutual aid as a primary strategy rather than a backup plan effectively means slower response times built into the system by design.

## Weighing the Cost of Paid Coverage

Facing these pressures, some towns have begun exploring contracts with private paid ambulance companies to guarantee coverage during the hours volunteer availability is weakest, typically weekday daytime hours. The shift carries a significant cost, one that smaller towns with limited tax bases have found difficult to absorb without raising local taxes or seeking state assistance.

Town officials who have studied the issue describe a difficult balancing act between fiscal constraints and public safety obligations. Several towns have opted for hybrid models, contracting for paid coverage during specific gap hours while continuing to rely on volunteers for nights and weekends when availability tends to be stronger.

## A Regional Conversation With No Easy Answers

State lawmakers representing the region have held informal discussions about potential funding mechanisms to support struggling volunteer squads, including grants for training costs and equipment, though no comprehensive statewide solution has emerged. Emergency management officials note that the challenge is not unique to Connecticut, reflecting a national decline in volunteer EMS participation that has affected rural communities across the country.

For residents of the Quiet Corner, the debate carries a personal weight that goes beyond budget line items. Many grew up with the understanding that a neighbor, someone from down the road who happened to have EMT training, would be the one showing up in an emergency. As that model becomes harder to sustain, towns are being forced to reckon with what they are willing to pay, and what tradeoffs they are willing to accept, to keep response times where they need to be.
