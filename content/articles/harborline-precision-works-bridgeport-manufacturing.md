The building on Bridgeport's East Side had sat mostly empty for three years when Teresa Okafor first walked through it in 2015, stepping around water damage and outdated equipment left behind by a machine shop that had closed during the recession. "Everyone told me I was buying a headache," Okafor said, standing now in the same space, transformed into a humming production floor lined with computer-controlled milling machines. "I saw a building full of skilled labor that had nowhere to go. That felt like an opportunity, not a headache."

That opportunity became Harborline Precision Works, a contract manufacturer specializing in precision-milled metal components for medical device makers and aerospace suppliers. Okafor, a mechanical engineer who had spent a decade managing production lines for a larger manufacturer before striking out on her own, rebuilt the shop's client base almost entirely from scratch, starting with small orders from local medical device startups before landing contracts with larger regional suppliers.

## Rebuilding Trust With Former Workers

One of Okafor's first moves was rehiring several machinists who had lost their jobs when the original shop closed. "They knew the equipment, they knew the building, and honestly they needed the work," she said. "It wasn't purely sentimental. It was good business." That decision helped Harborline get back into production faster than a from-scratch hire would have allowed, though Okafor acknowledges the early years required patience as she rebuilt trust with a workforce that had been burned by a previous shutdown.

Ray Delgado, Harborline's production floor supervisor and one of the machinists Okafor rehired, said the difference in management style was immediate. "The old ownership treated us like line items," Delgado said. "Teresa walks the floor every morning. She knows what's happening on every machine." Harborline now employs 74 people across two shifts, with plans to add a third shift as order volume from medical device clients continues to grow.

## Precision as a Competitive Edge

Harborline's growth has come from specializing in tolerances tighter than many competitors are willing to guarantee, a niche that requires continual investment in equipment calibration and staff training. Okafor says the company reinvests roughly 15 percent of annual revenue into new machinery and quality-control systems, a figure she considers non-negotiable even in leaner years. "In this business, your reputation is your tolerance spec," she said. "One bad batch and a client doesn't come back."

## Betting on Connecticut's Manufacturing Revival

Okafor has become an outspoken advocate for Bridgeport's manufacturing sector, testifying before a state legislative committee last year in support of expanded technical training funding. She argues that Connecticut's aging manufacturing workforce represents both a risk and an opportunity for cities like Bridgeport, and Harborline has partnered with a regional vocational school to create a machinist apprenticeship program that guarantees graduates an interview.

"We lost a generation of manufacturing jobs to the idea that this work was disappearing," Okafor said. "It didn't disappear. It just needed someone willing to reinvest in it." Harborline recently signed its largest contract to date, a multi-year supply agreement with a Connecticut-based aerospace component maker, and Okafor says she is already scouting additional floor space nearby. "This building gave me a second chance," she said. "I'd like to give this neighborhood one too."
