Primary care offices across Fairfield County are trying a mix of reminder calls, text alerts, and simplified scheduling to get patients back into the habit of an annual checkup, a routine visit that clinicians say too many adults have quietly let slide in recent years. Internal scheduling data reviewed by several local practices suggests that preventive visit rates have not fully rebounded to pre-pandemic levels, even as other categories of care have largely returned to normal.

The gap concerns physicians because annual exams often serve as the entry point for catching problems early, from elevated blood pressure and cholesterol to the kind of subtle changes that can signal a developing chronic condition long before symptoms would otherwise prompt someone to seek care.

## A Quiet Decline in Routine Visits

Unlike urgent or acute care, missed annual physicals rarely produce an immediate, visible consequence, which physicians say is part of why the decline has been so easy for patients to overlook. A family medicine physician in Stamford said she has noticed more patients arriving with conditions that likely could have been caught earlier had they kept up with routine visits.

"We're seeing patients come in for something else entirely and discovering blood pressure or blood sugar numbers that have clearly been elevated for a while," she said. "If they'd been in for their annual visit, we probably would have caught this a year or two earlier, when it's easier to manage."

Scheduling friction appears to be part of the problem. Many primary care practices in the region report booking windows stretched several months out for new physical exam appointments, a wait time that can cause patients to simply put off scheduling altogether, especially if they feel generally healthy in the moment.

## Making Preventive Care Easier to Access

In response, several practices have begun experimenting with expanded appointment types specifically reserved for annual physicals, distinct from the general sick-visit slots that tend to fill quickly with same-day requests. Some offices have also extended hours on select evenings and weekends to accommodate patients who struggle to take time off during a standard workday.

Digital outreach has become a bigger part of the strategy as well, with automated reminder systems now flagging patients who are overdue for a checkup based on the date of their last visit. Practice administrators say the reminders alone have prompted a noticeable uptick in scheduling calls, though staff acknowledge that reminders only work for patients who are already engaged enough with the healthcare system to receive and act on them.

## Insurance Coverage and Remaining Barriers

Most insurance plans, including Medicare and Medicaid, cover annual preventive visits at no out-of-pocket cost to the patient, a fact physicians say is not always well understood. Practices have started including plain-language explanations of coverage in their outreach materials, hoping to remove any lingering assumption that a checkup will come with a surprise bill.

Even with these efforts, some barriers remain stubborn, particularly for patients without reliable transportation or those working jobs with inflexible schedules that make daytime appointments difficult. Physicians say closing the remaining gap will likely require continued investment in flexible scheduling and outreach, paired with broader public messaging about the value of catching problems early rather than waiting for symptoms to force the issue. For now, providers across the region describe the push as a slow, visit-by-visit effort to rebuild a habit that slipped for many during a disruptive few years.
