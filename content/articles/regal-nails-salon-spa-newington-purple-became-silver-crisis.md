# The “Purple Became Silver” Crisis: When an 11:30 Appointment Turns Into a Two-Hour Wait — and the Customer Still Doesn’t Get What She Asked For

The customer asked for one nail to be purple.

She walked out with it silver.

Normally, a single incorrect color could be corrected in minutes.

But according to a customer review involving **Regal Nails, Salon & Spa in Newington**, by the time she noticed the problem, she had already spent more than **two hours** at the salon after waiting over an hour despite having an **11:30 appointment**.

She was done waiting.

So she left unhappy.

The reviewer even acknowledges that the salon was busy.

Her frustration was not simply that she had to wait.

Her question was more fundamental:

**If an appointment still means waiting more than an hour, what was the point of making one?**

Add a rushed-feeling service and a simple color request that was not followed, and the salon turned what could have been a minor scheduling delay into the loss of a customer.

## The Appointment Was at 11:30

Appointments create expectations.

The customer organizes her day around a specific time.

She drives to the salon.

Arrives.

Checks in.

And reasonably expects the service to begin somewhere near the scheduled appointment.

That does not mean salons can never run late.

A previous service may take longer than expected.

A technician may encounter a difficult correction.

Someone may call out.

Unexpected problems happen.

But according to this reviewer, the delay exceeded **one hour**.

At that point, the appointment no longer felt like an appointment.

## “I Understand They Were Busy”

This sentence is important.

The reviewer gives the salon some grace.

She acknowledges that they were busy.

That tells management something useful:

**The customer was capable of accepting a reasonable explanation.**

She was not demanding a perfectly synchronized operation.

She understood that a busy salon can fall behind.

Her frustration came from the mismatch between the reservation and reality.

She had taken the extra step of making an appointment specifically to avoid this type of uncertainty.

## An Appointment Is a Two-Sided Commitment

When customers reserve a time, they are expected to respect it.

Arrive on time.

Cancel appropriately.

Notify the salon if plans change.

Sometimes salons even charge customers for late cancellations or no-shows.

That makes appointment integrity a two-sided responsibility.

If customers are expected to respect salon time, salons should also make reasonable efforts to respect customer time.

Otherwise the booking system begins feeling one-sided:

**The customer's appointment is a commitment.**

**The salon's appointment is a suggestion.**

That damages trust.

## If You Are an Hour Behind, Tell the Customer

Communication could have changed this experience significantly.

If management knows an 11:30 appointment cannot realistically begin until 12:30, the customer needs information.

Tell her.

Apologize.

Give an updated estimate.

Offer reasonable options.

For example:

“We're unfortunately running about an hour behind. I apologize. You're welcome to wait, or I can help you reschedule.”

Now the customer controls her time.

She might still be unhappy.

But she is not trapped in uncertainty.

## Then the Service Felt Rushed

There is an operational irony in this review.

The customer waited a very long time.

Then she felt the actual service was rushed.

That is one of the worst combinations a salon can create:

**Wait slowly. Serve quickly.**

From the customer's perspective, she absorbs the consequences of both problems.

She loses time because the salon is behind.

Then she loses attention because the technician is trying to catch up.

This is how scheduling problems become quality problems.

## Running Behind Cannot Become the Next Customer's Problem

When a technician starts an appointment an hour late, there is understandable pressure.

The next customer may already be waiting.

The schedule continues moving.

Now the technician wants to recover time.

But trying to “make up” an hour by rushing the current customer's service simply transfers the original delay forward.

The customer did not cause the schedule to fall behind.

She should not receive a reduced experience because of it.

## She Asked for One Purple Nail

Then comes the simplest part of the complaint.

The customer wanted **one nail purple**.

According to her, she left with **one nail silver**.

This was not an elaborate portrait.

Not complicated 3D artwork.

Not an unusual custom mix.

From the reviewer's perspective, it was simply a color request.

That makes the mistake feel particularly unnecessary.

After waiting so long, she could reasonably expect the salon to at least follow the basic design instruction she gave.

## Purple and Silver Became Bigger Than Colors

Technically, the difference between one purple nail and one silver nail may seem small.

Emotionally, it represents something larger:

**“Nobody listened to me.”**

This is why simple mistakes can create disproportionate frustration after a long wait.

If everything else had been perfect, the customer might say:

“Oh, I actually wanted that one purple.”

The technician fixes it.

Problem solved.

But after more than two hours, the customer's patience budget was already depleted.

The silver nail became the final confirmation that the appointment had gone wrong.

## She Could Have Asked Them to Fix It — But She Was Done

This is another important training point.

Management might respond:

“Why didn't she just ask us to change the silver nail?”

The review answers that question.

She says she had already been there for **more than two hours** and did not want to wait anymore.

That means the correction may have been technically easy but psychologically unavailable.

The salon had consumed too much of the customer's time.

By the time the final mistake appeared, the customer no longer wanted recovery.

She wanted to leave.

## A Five-Minute Fix Can Become Too Expensive

Businesses often think about corrections in labor terms.

Changing one nail might require only a few minutes.

So management thinks:

“We could have fixed that easily.”

But the customer calculates cost differently.

Five more minutes after a normal appointment?

Probably acceptable.

Five more minutes after spending more than two hours at the salon?

That may feel intolerable.

**The longer the initial failure lasts, the less appetite the customer has for additional recovery time.**

## The Final Quality Check Could Have Saved the Visit

Before the customer leaves, compare the result against the request.

One purple accent nail?

Check it.

Requested shape?

Check it.

Length?

Check it.

Design placement?

Check it.

A simple final question could potentially have caught this:

“Everything look right before you go?”

Better still, the technician should independently remember the service request.

If the agreed accent nail was purple and it is visibly silver, the customer should not have to become the final quality-control department.

## Management Perspective: Track Appointment Delay Before It Becomes Service Failure

Salon managers should know when the schedule is slipping.

If an appointment is running:

10 minutes behind.

20 minutes behind.

30 minutes behind.

60 minutes behind.

The response should not remain identical.

At some point, proactive intervention is required.

Reassign a technician if possible.

Update the customer.

Offer rescheduling.

Adjust future bookings.

Do something before the technician finally receives the customer already stressed and then feels pressure to rush.

Because appointment delays rarely remain only appointment delays.

They spread into the rest of the experience.

## Do Not Try to Recover Schedule Time Through the Customer

This case demonstrates the chain clearly:

**Salon becomes busy → appointment begins very late → technician/customer feel time pressure → service feels rushed → basic request is missed → customer refuses to spend more time correcting it → customer leaves permanently.**

The original operational problem was scheduling.

The final public complaint included quality and listening.

That is how one department's failure becomes everyone's failure.

## Media & Customer Experience Perspective

The customer actually gives the salon considerable benefit of the doubt.

She says she understands they were busy.

But she also points out something every appointment-based business needs to hear:

**“That is why I made the appointment.”**

She booked for 11:30.

According to her account, she waited more than an hour before being seen.

She eventually spent more than two hours at the salon.

The service then felt rushed.

She requested one purple nail.

She walked out with it silver.

And by the time she could have requested another correction, she simply did not want to spend another minute waiting.

So she left unhappy.

And she says she will not return.

That means the salon did not lose this customer because purple polish is difficult.

It lost her because several small operational failures formed one clear message:

**Her time and preferences did not feel important.**

The appointment did not protect her time.

The rushed service did not make up for the delay.

The final result did not follow her request.

And the easiest mistake of all to correct — one wrong-colored nail — survived because the customer had already exhausted the one resource the salon could not give back:

**Her time.**

That is the lesson.

When customers have already waited an hour longer than expected, precision becomes more important, not less.

Because after two hours, the difference between purple and silver is no longer just a color mistake.

It can become the final reason a customer decides there will not be a next appointment.
