Blood donation centers supplying hospitals across Connecticut are reporting one of their leanest summers in recent memory, a seasonal dip that blood bank staff say has become predictable but no less concerning each time it arrives. With schools out and many regular donors traveling, the steady stream of donations that keeps hospital blood supplies stable has slowed considerably, even as demand from scheduled surgeries and trauma cases continues largely unchanged.

Blood centers coordinating collection across the state say the timing is particularly difficult because summer also brings an uptick in trauma cases tied to car accidents, water sports, and outdoor recreation, meaning the months when supply drops are often the same months when unpredictable demand for transfusions rises.

## Why Summer Hits Supply So Hard

Much of the state's blood supply is collected through organized drives held at high schools, colleges, and workplaces, venues that go quiet during summer break. A blood drive coordinator who organizes collections across central Connecticut said the loss of school-based drives alone accounts for a significant portion of the seasonal shortfall.

"High schools and colleges are some of our most reliable donation sites during the school year," she said. "When those close for the summer, we lose a huge chunk of our regular collection volume all at once, and it takes real effort to make that up through community drives."

Vacation schedules compound the problem, as many of the most consistent repeat donors, who might otherwise give blood every eight weeks as eligibility rules allow, find their usual donation routine interrupted by travel or simply forget amid a less structured summer schedule.

## The Stakes for Patients

Hospitals rely on a steady blood supply not just for emergency trauma care but for a wide range of routine and scheduled procedures, including surgeries, cancer treatments, and care for chronic conditions like sickle cell disease that require regular transfusions. When supply runs low, hospitals may need to postpone elective procedures that require blood products to be on hand, a step medical staff describe as a last resort but one that becomes necessary when inventory falls below safe thresholds.

Certain blood types face particular pressure during shortages. Type O negative, the universal donor type used in emergencies when a patient's blood type is not yet known, is typically in the shortest supply relative to demand, since it can be given to patients of any blood type but only a small percentage of the population can donate it.

## Efforts to Bridge the Gap

To offset the summer decline, blood centers have ramped up efforts to organize community and workplace drives, partnering with employers, churches, and civic organizations willing to host collection events during the slower months. Mobile donation units have also been deployed to fairs, farmers markets, and other summer gatherings where organizers hope to catch potential donors who might not otherwise seek out a fixed donation site.

Staff emphasize that donating blood remains a quick process, typically under an hour including registration and a short recovery period, and note that first-time donors are especially needed given the gradual attrition of the existing donor base over time. With hospital demand unlikely to ease, blood center officials say closing the summer gap will depend heavily on whether enough residents are willing to make time for a donation appointment amid vacation season, a request they plan to keep making through public outreach in the weeks ahead.
