On a Tuesday morning at Core & Compass Pilates in Fairfield, a retired schoolteacher works through a series of controlled leg extensions on a reformer machine just a few feet from a college lacrosse player recovering from a shoulder injury. It is a pairing that studio owner Trent Ashworth said reflects exactly the client base he set out to build when he opened the studio three years ago.

"Pilates has this reputation as either a gentle rehab exercise for older adults or a niche fitness trend for a certain type of athlete," Ashworth said. "In reality, the core principles apply just as well to both groups. I wanted a studio that didn't have to choose."

## A Method Built on Precision

Ashworth, a former physical therapy assistant who trained extensively in classical Pilates methodology before opening Core & Compass, structures the studio's reformer classes around small group sizes, capping most sessions at six participants so instructors can give hands-on corrections throughout. He said this attention to form, rather than pace or intensity, is what distinguishes serious Pilates instruction from the faster-paced group classes that have become popular at some larger fitness chains.

"You can make a Pilates class feel like a cardio workout if you want to," Ashworth said. "But that's missing the point of the method. The value is in the precision, the control, the connection between breath and movement."

The studio's client roster includes several members of a local college's athletic programs, who use targeted sessions to address muscular imbalances that can contribute to injury, as well as a growing group of retirees managing conditions like osteoporosis or post-surgical rehabilitation.

## Building Trust Across Generations

Retired nurse Constance Whitfield, 71, said she began attending Core & Compass after a hip replacement left her hesitant to return to her previous exercise routine. Nearly two years later, she attends three classes a week and said the studio's instructors have been attentive to her specific physical limitations in a way she did not experience at a previous fitness studio.

"They ask before every class if anything has changed since the last session," Whitfield said. "That kind of attention makes a real difference when you're working around an injury history like mine."

Ashworth has also begun offering specialized sessions for local athletes during their off-seasons, working with coaches at a nearby high school to design programs targeting common sport-specific injury risks. He said the collaboration has helped position Core & Compass as a resource within the town's broader athletic community, rather than simply another fitness studio competing for the same pool of clients.

As the studio approaches its third anniversary, Ashworth said he is considering adding a second reformer room to accommodate growing demand, though he remains committed to keeping class sizes small. "The moment we sacrifice that individual attention to fit more people in the room, we've lost what makes this work in the first place," he said. Core & Compass currently offers classes six days a week and maintains a waitlist for its most popular morning sessions.
