At a clinic in Danbury, a physician turns her tablet toward a patient to show him a graph of his blood pressure readings from the past six months, pulled up in seconds from an updated digital portal that now consolidates records, test results, and messaging in a single app. The rollout, expanded across Western Connecticut in recent months, is part of a broader effort by hospital systems to give patients more direct access to their own health information.

The updated portal allows patients to view lab results shortly after they are finalized, request prescription refills, message their care team directly, and schedule many routine appointments without calling the office. Hospital administrators describe the changes as a response to patient demand for the kind of on-demand access that has become standard in banking and retail, applied now to healthcare.

## Convenience for Some, a New Barrier for Others

For many patients, particularly younger and more tech-comfortable users, the expanded portal has been welcomed as a long-overdue convenience. Being able to check a test result at 9 p.m. rather than waiting for a callback, or message a question to a nurse without sitting on hold, has changed how some patients interact with their care team day to day.

But the shift has also raised concerns among advocates for older adults and patients with limited internet access, populations that remain significant in parts of Western Connecticut's more rural towns. A patient advocate who works with senior centers in the region said she has fielded a steady stream of questions from older patients who feel left behind by the technology.

"Not everyone has a smartphone, and not everyone who has one is comfortable using it for something like medical records," she said. "Clinics need to make sure the old ways of doing things, calling the office, mailing a letter, are still available for people who need them."

## Balancing Automation With Access

Hospital officials say they have tried to address the gap by maintaining traditional phone scheduling and paper communication options alongside the new digital tools, and by training front-desk staff to help patients set up portal accounts during in-person visits. Some clinics have gone further, hosting drop-in sessions where staff walk elderly patients through the app step by step.

Even with those accommodations, adoption has varied significantly across the patient population, with usage rates notably lower among patients over 75 and among non-English speaking households, according to clinic staff who track portal engagement. Translation features built into the app have helped somewhat, though staff note that translated medical terminology does not always fully capture nuance that a human interpreter would convey.

## What Comes Next

Health system leaders say they view the portal expansion as an ongoing project rather than a finished product, with plans to add features such as integrated video visit scheduling and expanded language support in the coming year. They also say they are tracking whether the tool is actually reducing administrative burden on staff, an outcome that has proven mixed in early data, since message volume through the portal has, in some cases, simply replaced phone call volume rather than eliminating it.

For now, patients across Western Connecticut are adjusting to a system that increasingly assumes digital fluency, even as hospital staff work to make sure that assumption does not leave anyone without care.
