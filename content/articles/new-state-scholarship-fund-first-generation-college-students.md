A newly launched scholarship fund is aiming to reduce financial barriers for first-generation college students attending public and private institutions across Connecticut, with organizers hoping the effort will help close persistent gaps in college completion rates between first-generation students and their peers.

The fund, established through a combination of private philanthropic donations and matching state support, will provide need-based awards to eligible students at participating Connecticut colleges and universities, including several of the state's public universities and a handful of private institutions that have agreed to match a portion of awarded funds with their own financial aid resources.

## Addressing a Persistent Completion Gap

Higher education researchers have long documented that first-generation college students, those whose parents did not complete a four-year degree, face distinct challenges beyond tuition costs alone, including less familiarity with financial aid processes, campus resources, and the general expectations of college life. National data has consistently shown lower degree completion rates among first-generation students compared to their peers, even when controlling for academic preparation.

A fund administrator involved in the launch said the scholarship was designed specifically to address financial pressures that can force first-generation students to work excessive hours during the school year or take extended leaves of absence, both of which are associated with lower completion rates. "Money alone doesn't solve every barrier these students face, but it removes one that can otherwise force impossible choices between paying rent and staying enrolled full time," the administrator said.

## How the Fund Works

Eligible students can apply for awards through their institution's financial aid office, with funds intended to supplement, rather than replace, existing state and federal aid. Award amounts vary based on individual financial need and are renewable for up to four years provided recipients maintain satisfactory academic progress, a structure organizers say was deliberately designed to support students through graduation rather than simply easing the cost of a single year.

Participating institutions have also agreed to provide dedicated advising support for scholarship recipients, pairing funding with mentorship intended to help first-generation students navigate aspects of college life that can feel unfamiliar, from selecting courses to understanding how to seek help from professors during office hours.

## Student and Institutional Reaction

Early recipients of the scholarship describe the award as removing a significant source of stress that had shaped their initial college experience. One recipient, a sophomore at a Connecticut public university, said she had been working nearly thirty hours a week at an off-campus job before receiving the award, a schedule she said had made it difficult to keep up with coursework. "This isn't just about money," she said. "It's about actually having the time to be a student."

Financial aid administrators at participating institutions say they hope the fund will serve as a model for additional philanthropic investment in first-generation student support, noting that demand for need-based aid consistently outpaces available funding at most Connecticut colleges. A financial aid director at one participating university said the scholarship fund had already allowed the office to say yes to students who would otherwise have been left with unmet financial need.

Fund organizers say they intend to expand the number of participating institutions and available awards in future years, contingent on continued fundraising, and describe the initial launch as a starting point rather than a finished effort to address college affordability for Connecticut's first-generation students.
