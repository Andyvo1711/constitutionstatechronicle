Connecticut College is expanding its marine and coastal studies program, leaning into the New London campus's proximity to Long Island Sound to give students more direct field research opportunities rather than relying primarily on classroom instruction. Faculty overseeing the expansion say the goal is to make fieldwork a routine part of the undergraduate experience rather than an occasional supplement to lecture-based courses.

The program has added new coursework focused on coastal ecology, shoreline erosion, and the effects of changing water temperatures on local marine life, subjects that carry particular relevance for a state whose coastline has faced increasing pressure from storms and shifting weather patterns in recent years.

## Fieldwork as Core Curriculum

Faculty members involved in the redesign say the expanded program reflects a broader shift in how environmental sciences are taught nationally, with growing emphasis on direct observation and data collection over purely theoretical instruction. Students in the program now spend regular class time collecting water samples, monitoring shoreline changes, and cataloguing local species along stretches of the Connecticut coastline.

A faculty member who helped design the new coursework said the shift has changed how students engage with the material. "When you're standing in the water taking measurements yourself, the abstract concepts from a textbook suddenly have consequences you can see and feel," the professor said. "That tends to stick with students in a way that a slide deck alone never quite manages."

## Responding to Regional Environmental Pressures

The expanded program comes as coastal communities throughout Connecticut have grappled with erosion, flooding, and other consequences of a changing climate along the Sound. College officials say partnerships with regional environmental organizations have grown alongside the academic program, giving students opportunities to contribute to real monitoring efforts used by local conservation groups rather than working solely on hypothetical class projects.

Several students in the program have already presented findings from coastal monitoring projects at regional environmental science gatherings, an experience faculty say gives undergraduates a rare opportunity to see their coursework translate into research with practical relevance beyond the classroom.

## Preparing Students for Environmental Careers

Career services staff at the college report growing student interest in environmental science and policy careers, a trend they attribute partly to increased public attention on climate-related issues along the Eastern Seaboard. The expanded marine studies program is designed, in part, to prepare graduates for work with environmental consulting firms, government agencies, and nonprofit conservation organizations that are increasingly seeking candidates with hands-on fieldwork experience rather than classroom knowledge alone.

One senior in the program said the fieldwork component had been a deciding factor in her choice to attend Connecticut College over other liberal arts institutions. "I wanted a program where I wasn't just reading about the Sound, I was actually working in it," she said, describing weekly trips to monitor a stretch of shoreline near campus as some of the most valuable coursework of her college career.

College administrators say they plan to continue expanding field station infrastructure and equipment for the program in coming years, contingent on continued fundraising support. They describe the coastal setting as one of the institution's most distinctive academic assets, one that few inland liberal arts colleges can offer students in quite the same way, and one they intend to keep building on as interest in environmental science continues to grow among prospective students.
