Three years ago, Nadia Kowalski nearly closed Harbor & Thread Salon. Not because the Milford business lacked clients, but because she could not keep stylists. Four left within an eighteen-month stretch, each citing exhaustion from a commission structure that rewarded booking as many appointments as possible, regardless of the toll on the stylist behind the chair.

"I built the original schedule around maximizing revenue per chair, which sounds smart on paper," Kowalski said. "What it actually did was burn out the people I depended on most."

## Rethinking Compensation and Pace

Kowalski, who bought the salon from its previous owner in 2016, spent months in early 2023 redesigning the salon's entire operating structure. She capped the number of appointments each stylist could book per day, introduced mandatory fifteen-minute breaks between certain services, and shifted a portion of compensation from pure commission to a hybrid model that includes a guaranteed base pay.

"It meant the salon made less money per stylist per day, at least on paper," Kowalski said. "But we stopped losing trained stylists every few months, and replacing someone is far more expensive than the commission we gave up."

The changes extended to how new stylists are brought into the salon. Harbor & Thread now pairs every new hire with a senior stylist for a structured six-week mentorship period, rather than the informal shadowing arrangement the salon previously relied on. Senior stylist Odalys Ferreira, who has worked at Harbor & Thread for seven years and now mentors incoming staff, said the structured approach has made a measurable difference.

"Before, a new stylist might get thrown onto the floor with barely any support," Ferreira said. "Now they have someone checking in with them every day for six weeks. It changes how confident they feel, and it changes how they treat clients."

## Retention as a Business Strategy

Since implementing the changes, Kowalski said Harbor & Thread has retained every stylist hired over the past two years, a reversal she attributes directly to the restructured schedule and compensation model. She has also noticed downstream effects on client relationships, since clients who previously had to switch stylists repeatedly due to turnover are now able to build longer-term relationships with a consistent stylist.

Client Wendell Ashby, who has been coming to the salon for haircuts for nearly five years, said the change was noticeable even without knowing the details behind it. "I used to get a new person every year or so," Ashby said. "The last three years, I've seen the same stylist every time. It makes a difference in how comfortable I feel just walking in the door."

Kowalski said she has begun sharing her approach with other independent salon owners in the region, several of whom have reached out after hearing about Harbor & Thread's retention numbers through local industry circles. "This isn't a secret formula," she said. "It's just prioritizing the people actually doing the work over squeezing every possible dollar out of a chair. I wish someone had told me that when I first took over the salon."
