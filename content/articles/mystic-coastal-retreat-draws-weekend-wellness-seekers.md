Even in the cooler months, small groups gather on the shoreline near Tidewater Wellness Retreat in Mystic before sunrise, moving through a slow sequence of yoga poses as the tide comes in. Founder Elowen Bramwell said the ritual, part of the retreat's signature weekend program, is precisely the kind of experience she set out to create when she left a corporate wellness consulting career to open the retreat four years ago.

"There's something about doing this work near the water that changes people's relationship to it," Bramwell said. "I could run the same sequence in a studio, and it would be a good class. Here, it becomes something people remember for years."

## A Weekend Format Built Around Rest

Tidewater's core offering is a two-night weekend program that combines beachside yoga sessions, guided cold-water immersion in Fishers Island Sound, and unstructured rest time, a deliberate departure from the packed itineraries common at many wellness retreats. Bramwell said she designed the schedule after noticing that guests at other retreats she had attended often left more exhausted than when they arrived.

"So many retreats are just a vacation with a wellness label slapped on, packed with back-to-back activities," Bramwell said. "We built long stretches of unscheduled time into the program on purpose. Rest has to be an actual ingredient, not just a word on the brochure."

The cold-water immersion sessions, led by a trained facilitator who guides guests through breathing techniques before entering the water, have become one of the retreat's most talked-about offerings, drawing both curious first-timers and experienced cold-water practitioners from around the region.

## Drawing Visitors From Beyond Connecticut

While many of Tidewater's guests come from within Connecticut, Bramwell said a growing share now travel from New York, Massachusetts, and as far as Pennsylvania, drawn largely through word of mouth and a modest but active social media presence. The retreat currently accommodates small groups of no more than sixteen guests per weekend, a cap Bramwell has maintained deliberately even as demand has grown.

Guest Theodora Lindqvist, who traveled from Boston for a weekend session last fall, said the intimacy of the small group size stood out compared to larger retreat programs she had attended previously. "You actually get to know the other people there," Lindqvist said. "By the second day, it didn't feel like a program with strangers. It felt like a small community."

Bramwell said she has resisted pressure to expand capacity or add additional weekend dates, citing concerns about diluting the quality of the experience and the toll additional programming would take on her small staff. She is, however, exploring a shorter single-day format aimed at local residents who want a taste of the retreat's approach without committing to a full weekend.

"Mystic has such a specific quality of light and water that I think people underestimate until they experience it slowly," Bramwell said. "My goal isn't to scale this into something bigger. It's to protect what makes it work in the first place."
