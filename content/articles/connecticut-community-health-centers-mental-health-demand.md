Waiting rooms at community health centers across Connecticut have grown noticeably fuller in recent months, and clinic directors say much of the increase is coming from a single department: behavioral health. Requests for counseling appointments have climbed steadily, pushing some clinics to extend hours and rework how they triage new patients.

The pattern is showing up in cities and smaller towns alike, from New Britain to Willimantic, according to clinic administrators who track appointment data. Many point to a combination of factors, including lingering effects of pandemic-era isolation, economic pressure on working families, and a slow but steady reduction in the stigma that once kept people from seeking counseling in the first place.

## Longer Waits, New Approaches to Triage

At several centers, the wait for a first appointment with a therapist has stretched to several weeks, a gap that clinic leaders say is uncomfortable given how quickly a mental health crisis can escalate. In response, some clinics have introduced same-day crisis slots reserved specifically for patients in acute distress, while routing less urgent requests into a longer queue.

A licensed clinical social worker who oversees intake at one Hartford-area clinic said the triage system has helped ensure the most urgent cases are seen quickly, but acknowledged it does not solve the underlying capacity problem. "We're seeing more people ask for help, which in some ways is a good sign," she said. "But we don't have enough therapists to meet that demand as fast as we'd like."

To stretch limited staff further, several clinics have expanded group therapy offerings, particularly for common concerns like anxiety, grief, and substance use recovery. Group sessions allow a single clinician to see more patients per hour than individual therapy, and many participants report finding value in hearing from others navigating similar struggles, though clinic staff are careful to note that group formats are not a substitute for individual care in every case.

## A Statewide Workforce Shortage

The rising demand has collided with a persistent shortage of licensed behavioral health providers in Connecticut, a problem that predates the recent surge in appointment requests. Community health centers, which often pay less than private practices or hospital systems, have struggled to compete for a limited pool of licensed clinicians, particularly in rural counties where few therapists set up independent practices.

Some clinics have turned to telehealth to widen their reach, allowing therapists to see patients across a broader geographic area without requiring travel. Others have partnered with graduate programs in social work and counseling psychology to bring in supervised trainees who can see patients under a licensed clinician's oversight, a model that helps expand capacity while also building the state's future workforce.

## What Comes Next

State health officials and clinic administrators have discussed a range of proposals to address the shortage, including loan forgiveness programs aimed at behavioral health providers willing to work in underserved areas and streamlined licensing pathways for clinicians trained out of state. None of these measures offer immediate relief, and clinic directors say they expect waitlists to remain a challenge through the year.

For patients navigating the system now, staff encourage reaching out even if a first appointment feels far off, noting that crisis lines and same-day slots remain available for urgent needs. As one clinic administrator put it, the goal is to make sure no one falls through the cracks while the broader workforce shortage slowly gets addressed.
