Inside the quiet upper floors of Trinity College's library in Hartford, a small team of archivists and student workers has spent the better part of two years photographing, scanning, and cataloguing thousands of pages from the college's rare book and manuscript collection. That work has now culminated in an expanded digital archive, giving researchers and members of the public online access to materials that were previously available only through in-person appointments.

The collection includes early printed books, handwritten correspondence, and regional historical documents that librarians say had been underused simply because so few people knew they existed or had the ability to travel to Hartford to view them. Digitization, library officials argue, removes that barrier almost entirely.

## From Locked Cabinets to Open Access

Special collections librarians describe the project as long overdue. Many of the materials had sat in climate-controlled storage for decades, handled only occasionally by visiting scholars or students working on specialized thesis projects. A library administrator who helped lead the digitization effort said the goal was to make the collection genuinely useful rather than simply preserved. "A rare book that nobody can see isn't really serving its purpose," the administrator said. "Digitization lets us protect the physical object while still letting people actually engage with what's inside it."

The project prioritized materials with strong regional historical value, including correspondence related to early Connecticut civic life and printed materials documenting the college's own nineteenth-century founding era. Librarians say these materials have already drawn interest from local historical societies and genealogy researchers who previously had limited means of accessing them.

## Student Involvement in the Process

Undergraduate and graduate student workers played a substantial role in the digitization process, learning specialized handling techniques for fragile materials alongside cataloguing and metadata standards used by research libraries nationally. Several students involved described the work as unexpectedly hands-on for a project centered on old paper and ink.

One student worker, a history major who spent two semesters on the project, said handling centuries-old documents changed how she thought about historical research. "You read about primary sources in class, but actually holding one, even carefully, with gloves, under supervision, makes the past feel a lot less abstract," she said.

## Wider Access, Wider Use

Faculty across several departments say they plan to incorporate the newly digitized materials into coursework, allowing students in courses on early American history or the history of the book to examine primary sources without requiring a library visit. Professors teaching such courses in prior years often relied on printed facsimiles or secondhand descriptions of materials that students can now view directly online.

Library officials say the digitization project will continue in phases, with additional materials added to the online archive as funding and staff time allow. They note that digitization is a labor-intensive process, particularly for fragile or oversized items that require specialized equipment to photograph without causing damage.

The expanded archive is publicly accessible through the library's website, and officials say early usage data suggests interest extending well beyond the campus community, including researchers from other New England institutions and members of the public with family or historical ties to the region. For a small liberal arts college, administrators say the project represents an outsized contribution to how historical materials from Connecticut's past can be studied and understood going forward.
