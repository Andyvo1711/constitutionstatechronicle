At Gilded Hands Nail Studio, a narrow storefront wedged between a coffee roaster and a tailor on Bedford Street, owner Yasmin Okafor keeps a small whiteboard behind the front desk listing which of her technicians are certified in which specialties. It is an unusual bit of transparency for a nail salon, and it reflects the studio's central pitch: technical skill first, trends second.

"There's a lot of pressure in this industry to chase whatever is going viral," Okafor said. "But most of what goes viral isn't actually good for the nail. I built this business around the idea that a manicure should look good in six months, not just in the photo you take when you leave."

## A Focus on Nail Health

Okafor, who worked as a manicurist in Manhattan for nearly a decade before relocating to Stamford in 2018, requires every technician at Gilded Hands to complete continuing education in nail anatomy and product chemistry, a step beyond the state's standard licensing requirements. She said the investment has paid off in fewer client complaints about lifting gel or damaged nail beds, a common complaint she heard frequently in her early years in the industry.

"When I started training my staff this way, some of them thought I was overcomplicating a simple service," Okafor said. "But clients notice the difference. Their nails grow out healthier, and they come back."

The studio's most requested service, a "structured manicure" that uses a light gel overlay to reinforce weak or thin nails without artificial tips, has become something of a signature offering. Client Renata Dubois, a Stamford schoolteacher who has been coming to Gilded Hands for two years, said she switched salons after years of damage from acrylics elsewhere.

"My nails used to be paper thin by the time I got them redone every few weeks," Dubois said. "Yasmin's team actually fixed that instead of just covering it up."

## Growing Without Losing Focus

Gilded Hands has expanded modestly since opening, adding a second treatment room last year and bringing on two additional technicians, but Okafor has resisted pressure to franchise or open a second location, citing concerns about maintaining consistent quality across sites.

"I've had people approach me about opening a second Gilded Hands in Greenwich or Norwalk," she said. "I'm not against growth, but I'm not going to put my name on a location I can't personally oversee the training at."

The studio has also begun hosting monthly workshops for its clients on at-home nail care, covering topics like cuticle maintenance and product ingredients to watch for. Okafor said the sessions are partly educational and partly a way of building trust with clients who might otherwise be tempted by cheaper, faster options nearby.

"Stamford has no shortage of nail salons," she said. "What we're competing on isn't price. It's whether someone trusts us with their hands." Gilded Hands Nail Studio is open six days a week and typically books appointments about two weeks out during peak spring and summer seasons.
