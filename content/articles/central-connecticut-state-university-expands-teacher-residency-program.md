Central Connecticut State University is expanding a teacher residency program that pairs aspiring educators with experienced mentor teachers in high-need school districts for a full academic year, an approach education faculty say better prepares new teachers for the realities of the classroom than traditional short-term student teaching placements.

The program, which has operated on a smaller scale for several years, places residents in partner districts across central Connecticut, where they gradually take on more classroom responsibility over the course of the year while working alongside an experienced teacher. University officials say the expansion will roughly double the number of residency placements available starting in the coming academic year.

## Rethinking Traditional Student Teaching

Education faculty who helped design the residency model say it responds to long-standing criticism that traditional student teaching placements, often lasting only a semester, do not give aspiring teachers enough time to develop classroom management skills or build relationships with students before being evaluated and certified. The residency model instead immerses candidates in a single classroom for a full year, allowing them to experience a complete arc of the school calendar, from the beginning-of-year routines to state testing season.

"You learn things in April that you simply can't learn in October," said a faculty member who coordinates the program. "A full year lets residents see how a classroom community develops over time, not just a snapshot."

## Addressing Statewide Teacher Shortages

The expansion comes as Connecticut school districts, like many across the country, have reported difficulty filling certain teaching positions, particularly in special education, bilingual education, and secondary math and science. University officials say the residency program has been designed with an eye toward steering more candidates into these high-need certification areas, in part by offering additional stipend support to residents who commit to pursuing those specializations.

District officials who have partnered with the program say residents often make a smoother transition into full-time teaching roles than educators trained through shorter placements. A human resources administrator at one partner district said the district has hired several former residents directly into open positions, citing their familiarity with the district's students, curriculum, and school culture as a significant advantage over external candidates.

## The Resident Experience

Current residents describe the year-long placement as demanding but formative. One resident working in a partner district's middle school said the extended timeline allowed her to build trust with students in a way she doubted a shorter placement would have permitted. "By the spring, these were my students," she said. "I wasn't a visitor in someone else's classroom anymore."

Mentor teachers involved in the program also play a significant role, receiving additional compensation and training in coaching techniques to help them support residents effectively. Several mentor teachers say the arrangement benefits their own practice as well, describing the process of articulating classroom decisions to a resident as a useful exercise in reflecting on their own teaching methods.

University officials say the expanded program will require additional coordination with partner districts and increased funding for resident stipends, which they note are essential to making the year-long commitment financially feasible for candidates who might otherwise need to hold outside jobs. Administrators say they view the investment as directly tied to addressing Connecticut's teacher pipeline challenges, particularly in districts that have historically struggled to attract and retain qualified educators.
