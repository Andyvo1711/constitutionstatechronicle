In a quiet room set aside from the usual bustle of a pediatric clinic, a small group of teenagers sit in a loose circle, eyes closed, following a guided breathing exercise led by a counselor trained in mindfulness-based techniques. The session is part of a new program designed to give adolescents dealing with anxiety practical tools they can use outside the clinic, whether that means calming down before a test or managing a racing mind at bedtime.

The program reflects a broader shift in pediatric mental health care toward integrating structured wellness practices alongside more traditional talk therapy and, where appropriate, medication. Clinicians involved in designing the curriculum say mindfulness techniques, drawn from practices with roots in meditation traditions but adapted into a secular, evidence-based format, have shown promise in helping teenagers build skills for managing stress in the moment rather than only processing it after the fact in a therapy session.

## Why Mindfulness for Teens, and Why Now

Rates of anxiety diagnoses among adolescents have climbed steadily over the past decade, a trend that mental health providers attribute to a mix of factors including academic pressure, social media use, and disrupted routines during the pandemic years that have proven slow to fully normalize for some young people. Traditional therapy remains the backbone of treatment, but clinicians say waitlists for individual counseling have grown long enough that many practices are looking for complementary approaches that can help patients in the meantime.

A clinical psychologist who helped design the program said mindfulness training fills a specific gap. "Talk therapy helps kids understand why they feel anxious and work through underlying issues over time," she said. "Mindfulness gives them something to actually do in the fifteen minutes before a big test, when the anxiety is happening right now and they need a tool, not an appointment."

Sessions typically combine guided breathing exercises, brief body-scan meditations, and discussion of how to apply these techniques in everyday situations like classroom presentations or social conflicts. The program is structured as a series of weekly group sessions rather than a one-time workshop, an approach designed to build the kind of consistent practice that research suggests makes mindfulness techniques more effective over time.

## Group Format Offers Added Benefits

Beyond the specific techniques taught, clinicians say the group format itself provides value that individual sessions cannot easily replicate. Teenagers in the program often discover that peers they might never have suspected are also struggling with anxiety, a realization that can reduce the isolation that frequently accompanies mental health struggles at that age.

One participant, a high school sophomore who asked that her name not be used, described the group as a relief. "I thought I was the only one freaking out about stuff other people seemed fine with," she said. "Hearing other kids talk about the same kind of worried thoughts made it feel less like something wrong with just me."

## Measuring Impact and Expanding Access

Program organizers are tracking participant-reported anxiety symptoms before and after completing the multi-week series, though they caution that formal outcome data is still being compiled and the program remains relatively new. Early anecdotal feedback from both participants and their families has been positive enough that clinic leaders are exploring how to expand capacity, including training additional staff to lead sessions and considering whether a similar format could work for younger children with appropriate age adjustments.

Access remains a challenge, as demand for the program has already outpaced available group slots at its current scale. Clinic administrators say expanding the offering will depend on securing additional funding and staff training, a familiar constraint across pediatric mental health services statewide, but one they say is worth pursuing given the response from families so far.
