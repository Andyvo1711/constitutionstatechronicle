Priya Anand still remembers the exact spreadsheet that convinced her to quit her job. It was 2017, she was three years into a data analyst role at a New Haven biotech firm, and she had just spent a weekend building a tool that automated a report her team usually took a week to compile. "I showed it to my manager and he asked if I could sell it to other departments," Anand said. "That was the moment I realized this could be a company, not just a side project."

That side project became Elm City Analytics, a software firm now headquartered in a converted warehouse space near Wooster Square. The company builds data-integration tools for hospital systems and regional insurers, helping organizations stitch together records scattered across incompatible software platforms. What started as Anand working alone from a shared desk has grown into a 48-person team serving clients across Connecticut, New York, and New Jersey.

## Solving a Problem Nobody Wanted to Own

Anand describes healthcare data integration as "the unglamorous plumbing nobody wants to fix." Hospital systems often run on decades-old software that does not communicate cleanly with newer platforms, leaving administrators to reconcile records manually or pay enormous licensing fees for proprietary connectors. Elm City Analytics built its reputation on middleware that translates between these systems without requiring clients to replace existing infrastructure.

"We're not trying to sell anyone a shiny new platform," Anand said. "We're trying to make the platforms they already have talk to each other." That pragmatic pitch resonated with budget-conscious hospital IT departments, and the company's first major contract, with a mid-sized New Haven-area health network, became a reference case that fueled years of subsequent growth.

## Building a Team in a Competitive Market

Recruiting technical talent to New Haven, rather than Boston or New York, has been one of Anand's persistent challenges. She has leaned on partnerships with local universities, including a paid internship program that has become a primary hiring pipeline. Roughly a third of Elm City's current engineering staff started as interns, according to the company.

Dennis Whitcomb, the company's head of engineering, said Anand's insistence on staying local shaped the culture in ways that helped retention. "A lot of our engineers could make more money at a bigger firm in a bigger city," Whitcomb said. "But they like that decisions get made fast here and that Priya still reviews architecture proposals herself." Anand acknowledges the tradeoff but says proximity to New Haven's research institutions gives the company an edge in recruiting people who care about healthcare specifically, not just software generally.

## Expanding Beyond Healthcare

Elm City Analytics recently began adapting its integration tools for regional insurers seeking to modernize claims processing, a move Anand sees as a natural extension of the company's core expertise. The insurance pilot, launched with a mid-sized Connecticut carrier earlier this year, has already generated enough interest that Anand is weighing whether to spin the product into a separate business line.

Despite the growth, Anand insists the company will remain headquartered in New Haven. "There's a narrative that you have to leave Connecticut to build something ambitious," she said. "I want Elm City to be proof that isn't true." She has also started mentoring other local founders informally, hosting a monthly meetup for early-stage tech entrepreneurs in the company's conference room. "Somebody showed me that spreadsheet moment was possible," she said. "I want to be that person for somebody else."
