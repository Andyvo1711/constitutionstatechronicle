Twice a week, a conference room on the fourth floor of a downtown Bridgeport office building transforms into something closer to a fitness studio, with employees rolling out mats between desks for a lunchtime stretching and mobility session led by an instructor from Anchorline Wellness Partners. It is a small but visible sign of a shift underway among several mid-sized employers in the city, who are increasingly bringing wellness programming directly into the workplace rather than relying solely on gym membership stipends.

"For years, the standard benefit was just a discounted gym membership that maybe fifteen percent of employees actually used," said Anchorline founder Desmond Achterberg, whose company contracts with several Bridgeport businesses to run on-site programming. "Bringing the instructor into the building removes almost every barrier to participation."

## Meeting Employees Where They Are

Anchorline's programs range from guided stretching and breathing sessions to more structured strength training classes held before or after standard work hours, tailored to each employer's space constraints and workforce needs. Achterberg said the company's most requested offering among office-based clients is a midday mobility session designed specifically to counteract the physical toll of prolonged sitting.

"We hear the same complaints across almost every office client: tight hips, shoulder tension, headaches from screen time," Achterberg said. "The sessions we run are built around those specific, common complaints rather than a generic workout."

Human resources director Felicity Okonjo, who oversees benefits at a logistics company that began contracting with Anchorline last year, said the decision followed a noticeable increase in employee absences linked to reported back and neck pain. "We looked at our health claims data and saw a pattern," Okonjo said. "It made a compelling case that a modest investment in prevention could reduce both discomfort and downstream costs."

## Measuring Results Beyond Attendance

Since introducing the program, Okonjo said her company has tracked both participation rates and, informally, employee feedback through periodic surveys. Early results suggest participating employees report fewer instances of discomfort-related absences, though she cautioned that the sample size remains too small to draw firm conclusions after less than a year of programming.

Employee Rutger Vance, a warehouse operations coordinator who attends the twice-weekly sessions, said the convenience of on-site programming made the difference in his own participation. "I would never go to a gym before or after a shift like this," Vance said. "But when it's in the building during lunch, there's really no excuse not to show up."

Achterberg said Anchorline has expanded its client roster in Bridgeport steadily over the past two years, though he acknowledged that convincing smaller employers to invest in the programming remains a challenge given the upfront cost compared to a standard gym stipend. He is currently developing a scaled-down version of the program aimed at smaller offices with fewer than twenty employees, hoping to make the approach accessible beyond larger companies.

"The employers who commit to this tend to see it pay off in ways that are hard to capture in a single metric," Achterberg said. "Fewer sick days, sure, but also just a workplace that feels like it's investing in its people."
