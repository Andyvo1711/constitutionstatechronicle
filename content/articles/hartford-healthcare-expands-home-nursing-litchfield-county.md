For residents of Connecticut's northwestern hills, a trip to the nearest full-service hospital can mean a 40-minute drive down winding two-lane roads, longer still when winter weather closes in. A newly expanded home nursing program is designed to shorten that distance, bringing registered nurses and care coordinators directly to patients' kitchen tables in towns like Norfolk, Cornwall, and Kent.

The program, which grew out of a smaller pilot launched in neighboring towns two years ago, now covers most of Litchfield County. Nurses make scheduled visits to check vital signs, manage medications, and monitor recovery after hospital discharge, reducing the need for follow-up trips that can be difficult for elderly or homebound residents to arrange.

## Filling Gaps in a Sparse Care Network

Litchfield County has long presented a challenge for Connecticut's healthcare systems. Its population is older than the state average, its towns are spread thin, and public transportation is scarce outside the larger boroughs. A nurse practitioner who coordinates visits in the region said the program was built specifically around those constraints.

"A lot of our patients simply can't get to a clinic easily, especially in the months after a hospital stay," she said. "Bringing the visit to them means we catch problems early, before someone ends up back in the emergency room."

Officials involved in the rollout say the expansion reflects a broader shift among Connecticut's hospital systems toward care delivered outside traditional facility walls. Rather than requiring patients to travel for routine monitoring, nurses now carry portable diagnostic equipment, including handheld ultrasound devices and wireless blood pressure cuffs, that sync data back to a patient's electronic chart in real time.

## Early Results and Local Reaction

Preliminary figures shared by program organizers suggest the home-visit model has reduced avoidable emergency department visits among enrolled patients, though officials caution that the sample size remains small and a fuller evaluation is still underway. Local officials in several Litchfield County towns have welcomed the expansion, noting that the region's volunteer ambulance corps has faced its own staffing pressures in recent years.

At a recent town meeting in Norfolk, several residents described the difficulty of managing chronic conditions like diabetes and congestive heart failure without easy access to specialists. One retired schoolteacher, who asked not to be named, said a nurse's regular visits after a hip replacement last fall likely prevented a rehospitalization.

"She caught swelling in my leg that I hadn't even noticed," the resident said. "Without that visit, I probably would have ended up back in Torrington."

## Staffing the Program Remains a Challenge

Recruiting nurses willing to spend much of their day driving between rural towns has proven harder than administrators initially expected. Program leaders say they have offered mileage reimbursement and flexible scheduling to attract candidates, and they are exploring partnerships with nursing schools in the region to build a pipeline of new hires familiar with rural practice.

The initiative also relies on a small fleet of shared vehicles equipped with medical supplies, coordinated through a scheduling system that groups visits by geography to minimize windshield time. Even so, some routes in the county's more remote corners can eat up more than an hour of driving for a single appointment.

Health system administrators say they plan to evaluate the program's impact on hospital readmission rates later this year and will consider extending a similar model to other rural stretches of the state, including parts of eastern Connecticut where hospital access is similarly limited. For now, families in Litchfield County say the extra set of eyes checking in at home has offered a measure of reassurance that was hard to come by before.
