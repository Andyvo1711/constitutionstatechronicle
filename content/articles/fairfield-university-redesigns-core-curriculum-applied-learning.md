Fairfield University has rolled out a revised core curriculum this semester that shifts emphasis away from traditional lecture-heavy general education requirements and toward applied, project-based coursework tied to real community problems. Faculty involved in the redesign describe it as the most significant change to the university's general education structure in more than a decade.

Under the new model, students are required to complete at least one project-based course that partners with an outside organization, ranging from local nonprofits to small businesses in the Fairfield and Bridgeport areas. Rather than submitting a final paper to a professor alone, students in these courses typically present findings or deliverables directly to the partner organization.

## Moving Beyond the Traditional Lecture Hall

University administrators say the redesign responds to years of feedback from both students and employers who felt that traditional coursework, while academically rigorous, did not always translate into practical skills employers were seeking. A curriculum committee spent more than a year studying peer institutions and consulting with faculty across departments before finalizing the new requirements.

"We heard consistently that students wanted to see how what they were learning connected to something outside the classroom," said a faculty member who chaired the redesign committee. "This isn't about abandoning rigor. It's about pairing rigor with relevance."

## Early Examples in Practice

In one of the first courses offered under the new model, business students worked with a Bridgeport nonprofit to develop a marketing plan for a job training program serving formerly incarcerated residents. In another, environmental science students partnered with a local land trust to survey invasive plant species along a protected greenway. Faculty overseeing these courses say the format requires more logistical coordination than a standard lecture course but tends to generate noticeably higher student engagement.

Students who completed early pilot versions of the applied courses last year describe the experience as more demanding but ultimately more memorable than typical general education requirements. "It's a lot easier to stay motivated when you know an actual organization is depending on your work being useful," said a junior communications major who worked on a nonprofit's social media strategy as part of a pilot course.

## Balancing Access and Logistics

The shift has not been without challenges. Faculty note that coordinating with outside community partners requires more administrative support than traditional courses, and some departments have had to scale back the number of sections offered in a given semester while partnerships are established. University officials say they are investing in a dedicated staff position to help faculty identify and manage community partnerships going forward, aiming to ensure the model remains sustainable as it expands to more departments.

Local organizations that have partnered with the university say the arrangement benefits both sides. A director at one Bridgeport nonprofit that worked with a marketing class said the students' work provided genuinely useful materials the organization would not otherwise have had the budget to produce. "It's not just free labor," the director said. "The students brought fresh thinking to a project we'd been putting off for years."

University leaders say they plan to expand the applied learning requirement to additional majors over the next several academic years, with the eventual goal of making community-engaged coursework a defining feature of a Fairfield undergraduate education rather than an optional add-on for particularly motivated students.
