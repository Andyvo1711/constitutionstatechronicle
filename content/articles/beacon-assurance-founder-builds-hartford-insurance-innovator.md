On the fourteenth floor of a glass-and-brick tower just off Asylum Street, Marcus Reinholt keeps a single memento from his first office: a folding table he used for eleven years before Beacon Assurance Group could afford anything sturdier. "I tell new hires that table is worth more to me than the mahogany desk downstairs," he said on a recent Tuesday, gesturing toward the Hartford skyline through his office window. "It reminds everyone here where we started."

Where Beacon started, in 2009, was a two-person claims-consulting operation helping small manufacturers untangle disputes with larger carriers. Reinholt, a former claims adjuster who grew frustrated watching business owners get outmaneuvered by insurers with far more resources, decided he could build something fairer. Seventeen years later, Beacon Assurance Group underwrites commercial property and casualty policies for more than 3,000 mid-market businesses across Connecticut, Massachusetts, and Rhode Island, with gross written premium that Reinholt says has grown by double digits in six of the past eight years.

## From Claims Advocate to Underwriter

The pivot from consulting to underwriting was not an easy one. Reinholt recalls spending nearly two years courting reinsurance partners who were skeptical of a founder with no traditional actuarial background. "I had people tell me to my face that I didn't belong in this business," he said. "I used that as fuel, honestly." Beacon eventually found its footing by specializing in a niche few larger insurers wanted: coverage for light manufacturers and specialty contractors, industries Reinholt understood intimately from his adjusting days.

That specialization has become the company's calling card. Beacon's underwriting team, now 62 people strong, builds policies around detailed site visits rather than generic industry codes, a practice Reinholt insists keeps loss ratios manageable even as premiums stay competitive. Frank Iovino, Beacon's chief underwriting officer, said the approach requires more legwork but pays off in retention. "Clients feel like we actually looked at their operation before pricing their risk," Iovino said. "That's rarer than it should be in this industry."

## Investing Back in Hartford

Reinholt has been vocal about keeping Beacon's headquarters and hiring concentrated in Hartford even as remote work reshaped the insurance sector. The company recently expanded its downtown footprint, adding a full floor to accommodate a growing claims division, and has partnered with two local community colleges to build an apprenticeship pipeline for underwriting assistants. "This city gave a claims guy with a folding table a shot," Reinholt said. "We're not going anywhere."

The firm has also weathered turbulence common to smaller regional insurers, including a stretch in 2019 when a cluster of weather-related claims tested its reserves. Reinholt credits conservative reinsurance treaties negotiated years earlier for keeping Beacon solvent through that period, a lesson he now builds into new employee training. "We plan for the bad years during the good ones," he said.

## Looking Toward the Next Decade

Beacon is currently exploring a expansion into cyber liability coverage for small businesses, a category Reinholt says is underserved among Connecticut's manufacturing and logistics clients. The company has hired two new specialists to build out that book of business and expects to write its first cyber policies later this year.

Colleagues describe Reinholt as demanding but personally invested in staff development, often sitting in on entry-level training sessions himself. "He still remembers what it felt like to be an outsider in this industry," Iovino said. "That shapes how he runs the place." For Reinholt, the folding table remains the point. "Every policy we write is for somebody who feels like the little guy," he said. "I never want to forget what that feels like."
